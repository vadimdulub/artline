## What the research changes

Decolonisation involves political independence, but also struggles over labour,
language, cultural authority and the right to represent everyday life. The
selection distinguishes direct images of oppression and resistance from works
that belong through their artistic context. It does not classify every work by
an African, Asian or Caribbean artist as anti-colonial.

**Colonial systems and their human consequences.** Gerard Sekoto’s workers
occupy the composition collectively, while an overseer stands apart. The
artist’s account, discussed by [Smithsonian Libraries](https://www.sil.si.edu/silpublications/modernafricanart/monographs_detail.cfm?artist=Sekoto%2C+Gerard%2C+1913-1993),
supports reading *The Song of the Pick* through labour, racial power and dignity.
Abedin’s drawings bring colonial Bengal into view through suffering and
vulnerability. [documenta’s research](https://www.documenta14.de/en/notes-and-works/24940/zainul-abedin-1914-1976-)
connects his famine practice to a longer history of public art institutions and
social commitment. The WikiArt sheets remain individually identified; they are
not assigned the dimensions or lenders of other drawings from the same series.

**Resistance and the contested history of conquest.** Orozco’s Dartmouth panels
challenge heroic accounts of conquest and expose relationships between force,
wealth and modern institutions. The [Hood Museum’s guide](https://hoodmuseum.dartmouth.edu/sites/hoodmuseum/files/orozcovisitorguide.pdf)
provides panel-specific readings. Siqueiros’s *América Tropical* attacked
imperial domination in Los Angeles and was subsequently whitewashed; its
recovery is part of its reception history, rather than a new creation date.
[Getty’s conservation account](https://www.getty.edu/projects/conservation-america-tropical/)
also makes clear that Getty was a project partner, not the owner of a painting
in its collection. Malangatana’s 1967 painting brings the selection into the
Mozambican independence struggle. The [Met’s exhibition labels, p. 95](https://libmma.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/api/collection/p16028coll12/id/18919/download)
document both the object and that historical connection.

**Who determines the centre of artistic life?** Torres-García’s inverted map
reorients cultural authority towards South America, as analysed by
[Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/torres-garcia-inverted-america/).
Tarsila’s *Abaporu* and *Anthropophagy* propose transforming external influences
into a Brazilian modernism; these are metaphors of cultural autonomy, not
ethnographic accounts of Indigenous peoples. [MoMA’s interpretation](https://www.moma.org/audio/735)
and [Pinacoteca’s account](https://pinacoteca.org.br/programacao/exposicoes/arte-no-brasil-galeria-jose-e-paulina-nemirovsky-arte-moderna/)
support that distinction. Hyppolite and Lam bring different Caribbean visual
worlds into the selection. [MoMA’s Hyppolite research](https://www.moma.org/collection/artists/2790)
is especially useful for resisting a story in which European recognition alone
confers artistic importance. Lam’s additional paintings are cultural context;
their inclusion does not assert that they depict a specific political event.

**Language, gender and artistic renewal after independence.** Chua Mia Tee’s
classroom and public recitation make everyday participation part of a national
story. The [National Gallery Singapore’s analysis](https://www.nationalgallery.sg/sg/en/learn-about-art/our-publications/digital-catalog/siapa-nama-kamu-exhibition-catalogue/chapters-overview/the-real-against-the-new-social-realism-and-abstraction.html)
explicitly connects Malay-language learning to anti-colonial change. Baya’s
post-1962 return to painting deserves space on its own terms. Her birds, plants
and musical instruments are not illustrations of battles; they participate in
a renewed artistic life after Algerian independence. [AWARE’s research](https://awarewomenartists.com/en/artiste/baya/)
also identifies the orientalist categories imposed on her reception. Melehi’s
abstraction broadens the account beyond figurative protest: [Centre Pompidou](https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/ressources/oeuvre/cGEEgpG)
connects *Pulsation* to his wave motif, calligraphy and later Moroccan cultural
activity, and records its inclusion in an exhibition about modern art and
decolonisation. The painting was made in New York; nationality is not used to
invent its place of creation.

## Source assessment and exclusions

- WikiArt supplied discovery, exact object links and reproductions. Museum and
  scholarly sources supplied historical interpretation and checked identities.
  Direct WikiArt HTML retrieval was unreliable. Where necessary, the batch
  uses retained 19 September profile captures with their original receipts,
  alongside current web retrievals; these are not falsely dated as new captures.
- Visual review excluded Abedin’s *Untitled, 1943*: the image shows crows and
  appears signed with a different year. It is not presented as a human famine
  scene. The source and rejected derivative remain archived. The existing
  *Famine Series, 1953* record was neither redated nor merged.
- WikiArt’s Orozco panel numbers 13 and 16 differ from Hood’s 11 and 14. Titles
  and dates follow the museum; alternative numbering remains in the evidence.
  The Cortez reproduction is labelled as an installation view with adjoining
  panels, not an isolated full-frame photograph of that panel alone.
- WikiArt’s *Zapata, 1930–1931* belongs to the Cuernavaca cycle. It was not
  attached to the existing *Agrarian Leader Zapata, 1931* portable fresco.
- A Hyppolite entry dated 1953 conflicts with the artist’s 1948 death; it was
  held. A Baya installation photograph from 2023 was not treated as a dated
  artwork. Post-1970 works and unknown creation dates were excluded.
- No holding is inferred from photo credits, conservation involvement or a
  general exhibition biography. Torres-García’s conflicting institutional
  references remain unresolved. Named creators without reconciled profiles
  remain object-level labels, with no invented biographies.
- National Gallery publications give different dimensions for *Epic Poem of
  Malaya*. The object-level Roots dimensions are retained with the discrepancy
  recorded. El-Salahi’s accessible Tate print page supplies the original title
  and date; print dimensions are not copied into original-work metadata.
