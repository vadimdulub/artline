The question for month one: can a focused invitation bring interested people into Artline, help them discover something, and give some of them a reason to come back?

Targets below are proposed learning goals. No current traffic, conversion, keyword-volume or subscriber baseline was available for this review.

Start with curious art-history learners.

Choose people who already spend time learning about art and want help connecting works to a period, place or artist. Start in English; use Greek and Byzantine material as the first topic to test.

This is an editorial choice based on Artline’s existing research and priorities, not a claim that this is the largest market. Greek museum coverage provides material to select from; it does not establish complete coverage or ready-made visitor itineraries.

“Explore art through time. Follow the artists, artworks, books and events that help you understand a period.”

Proposed core message. Use one concrete example immediately below it.

First readers

Independent learners, art-history students and museum enthusiasts. Their task: “Help me understand what I’m looking at and what to explore next.” Invite them to one short visual exploration.

First collaborators

Art-history teachers, small cultural newsletters and specialist creators. Their task: “Give my audience something useful to explore.” Offer a sourced comparison exercise before asking for a share.

Second audience to test

People researching Greek or Cypriot collections before a visit. Lead with documented holdings and official museum links. Validate this separately from general learning intent.

The distinction to demonstrate

Let people move from a work to its artist and historical context in one journey. Show the experience in a short screen recording. Avoid untested claims of superiority or completeness.

Scope remains selected works created in or before 1970, with unknown and crossing dates explicit. Greek, Russian-icon, Byzantine and post-Byzantine topics remain first-class content priorities.

Make the first five minutes rewarding.

Read one question

A guide explains what to look for, using three to five selected works.

Follow one connection

Open an artwork, then its artist or museum. Sources and uncertain facts stay visible.

Choose the next story

A related guide gives the visit a natural continuation and a reason to return.

  1. Give each campaign one destination. Begin with the existing timeline guide. Move a topic campaign to its dedicated guide once published. Check the final URL anonymously on a phone.
  2. Give every selected work a useful caption. Include title, qualified creator, known date, a supported observation, source and actual image credit. Similar dates or appearances alone do not establish influence.
  3. Check the exact path a new reader takes. Open the guide, work and next record without a signed-in session. If any step has a member gate, make it clear and choose an accessible campaign path while preserving access controls.
  4. Use an honest return invitation. Link the second guide. Offer email only after signup, confirmation and unsubscribe actually work. Treat bookmarks, collections and paid concepts as feature-specific claims that need live verification.

A 15-minute reader session

Ask what the reader last tried to learn about art. Give them the guide and ask them to find a work that interests them, then something related. Stay quiet while they try. Ask: “What did you learn?”, “Where did you get stuck?” and “When would you use this again?” Record the obstacle and one change, with permission for any quote.

Campaign selection is editorial focus. All active catalogue records remain available under the unified catalogue, including anonymous, undated and unillustrated works. Empty museum records do not become marketing destinations.

Launch with two guides people can use.

Keep the original topic ideas, but give the first two a clear job. These briefs are proposals; neither is claimed to be published or supported by measured keyword demand.

Guide 1 / week 1–2

How to look at a Byzantine icon: three things to notice

For the curious beginner. Select three to five existing, source-backed works. Explain a few visible details with object-specific evidence and qualified interpretations. End with one comparison question.

  1. A direct answer and a short explanation of the terms.
  2. Three illustrated observations with captions and sources.
  3. A comparison linking two exact artwork records and their context.
  4. One clear action: “Explore these works in Artline.”

Ready when: each claim has evidence, each image matches the object, each destination opens, and a first-time reader can explain one thing they noticed. If the relevant works lack adequate evidence, use the existing timeline guide while researching.

Guide 2 / week 2–3

Greek art across time: five works, five starting points

For someone who wants a manageable route into Greek art. Choose five eligible records with supported dates or clearly stated uncertainty. Explain their differences and show where the reader can continue.

  1. A compact chronology; unknown dates stay unplaced or qualified.
  2. One paragraph per work: what to notice, why it belongs here, where the evidence comes from.
  3. Artist, artwork and documented holding links where available.
  4. A return link to the first guide and a question for the next one.

Ready when: all five selections have documented relevance, working paths and honest captions. No “all Greek museums” or “see these today” claim.

Next: Russian icons

Develop a sourced visual comparison from actual reader questions. Keep anonymous and workshop attributions intact.

Next: collections in Cyprus

Introduce selected holdings with official collection links. Fresh museum information is required before adding visiting or current-display advice.

Reuse the research

Turn each guide into one five-panel visual story, a 30–45 second screen recording and one short discussion prompt. Each version should teach something on its own and link to the same relevant destination.

Choose length to answer the question; original explanation, clear sourcing and useful comparisons matter more than a word target. See Google’s people-first content guidance. Use informative alternative text and nearby captions for images: Google Images guidance.

A collaborator offer that is easy to assess

Prepare a one-page exercise: two works, a small timeline, three questions and source links. Offer it to one educator for a real class or reading group. Ask what they used and what confused learners. A share is optional; do not imply a museum or institution endorses Artline.

Track: person or organization, why they fit, relevant public contact route, resource offered, invitation date, reply, referral tag and outcome. One relevant follow-up is enough; stop on a decline.

Broader launch later: consider a maker directory such as Product Hunt only once the first-visit journey works and a demo is ready. Audience fit is still a hypothesis. Use its current launch guide when preparing that separate experiment.

Minimum measurement setup

No dedicated analytics or newsletter integration was found in the inspected application paths; external tooling may exist. First confirm the actual setup. Proposed events: guide opened, artwork opened and related record opened, carrying page/record IDs and campaign source. Avoid personal data in URLs or event payloads.

Use consistent tags, for example utm_source=instagram, utm_medium=organic_social, utm_campaign=first_readers, utm_content=icon_detail_1. Apply them to external campaign links; do not overwrite acquisition with internal-link tags. Confirm the selected analytics actually records them.

For channel comparisons, assign each measured visitor to their first observed launch source and preserve that cohort for return analysis. Record unattributed traffic separately; never sum overlapping platform reach into unique visitors.

People arrive but do not explore

If fewer than 20 of the first 100 qualified visitors activate, run five focused usability checks and improve the promise, next link or page speed before increasing distribution.

People explore but do not return

If fewer than four of at least 20 eligible activated readers return, test a clearer second story and ask why it was not useful enough to revisit.

One route shows repeat use

Give it half of next month’s distribution time after it brings at least 20 qualified visitors and three observed returns. Compare hours per activated visitor and return counts, not likes.

The sample is still small

Continue for two more weeks, improve recruitment and keep the audience fixed. Do not declare a channel successful or failed from a handful of clicks.

These are practical decision rules for a small experiment, not statistical significance tests. Review every Friday: counts, denominators, source, hours, one reader observation and one change for the coming week.

Protect time for conversations.

Default to 40 founder hours and €0 in advertising during the first month. Reserve up to €100 for optional tools only if a clear gap appears; this is a planning ceiling, not an instruction to spend or a vendor quote.

If only 5 hours a week are available

Make one guide, ten personal invitations and two visual posts. Keep the five reader sessions and weekly review. Shift Guide 2 to month two; do not drop feedback to preserve publishing volume.

If technical work is larger than expected

Start reader sessions on the current timeline guide. Keep analytics and a guide template as bounded tasks. Defer the newsletter until there is a reliable signup-to-delivery flow.

Days 31–60

Repeat the strongest audience/topic combination, publish the next two guides from actual questions and try a second educator collaboration. Reassess search pages over a longer window.

Days 61–90

If repeat use holds, interview ten returning readers about collections, notes or exports. Prototype the most repeated need and test willingness to pay. The old €5/month idea remains unvalidated.

Keep later work conditional. Affiliate programs, multiple social channels, a Product Hunt launch and paid acquisition each need evidence of fit. A future small ad experiment should have a fixed spending cap and an activated-visitor target based on observed conversion.

Catalogue growth continues under the existing source policies. Marketing does not require a complete national museum directory, bulk imports or new placeholder artworks.
