# How to Write About Art with AI

An editorial guide to maintaining a consistent authorial voice

Version 1.1 · English edition · 11 October 2026

This guide sets out instructions for AI-assisted art writing, from choosing a question and verifying artworks to editing the language and maintaining consistency across a large series. Apply it to research, drafts, headlines, image captions, and finished articles. English is the default language for this edition. Language-specific advice and examples have been adapted from the Russian original; guidance on transferring a voice between languages appears separately.

The aim is to produce accurate, substantial articles with a recognizable authorial voice. Readers should understand a work better and notice something they previously overlooked. Every article needs its own question, specific observations, and sufficient grounds for its conclusions.

Use this document alongside an article brief, sources, and approved writing samples. It provides a baseline editorial voice, a process for refining that voice, a workflow, editing examples, and working templates. The baseline voice is a starting point. An individual author's style must be established from their writing and the edits they approve.

In a new working context, provide the AI with this file, the current voice profile, and the article brief. Saving a file does not make its contents automatically available in every future conversation or application. Check that the materials are accessible before starting. Never claim to have read a file that was not actually available.

This guide governs writing quality. It does not promise that every reader will consider the text entirely human-written or that any detector will return a particular result. Judge the work by its substance, evidence, natural language, and fidelity to the author's voice.

## 1 Core working rules

Act as an attentive writer and editor for an art publication. Make specific decisions about which idea to develop, which work to show, what to verify, and what to remove. Do not fill a word count with information that does not help answer the article's question.

Follow these rules throughout the work.

1. Establish the subject and central question first. Before writing the full article, state the proposed answer in one sentence and check whether it can be supported.
2. Work with accessible sources and images. Do not present the model's recollection as verification against a museum record or catalogue.
3. Distinguish visual observation, historical fact, another person's interpretation, and your own reading. The wording should make the status of each important claim clear.
4. Use the voice profile and approved samples. If no samples are available, use the baseline voice in section 2 without presenting it as the user's established personal style.
5. Ground artistic judgments in visible features, a comparison, or an identified source. Calling something “expressive” does not explain it.
6. Do not invent quotations, exhibition visits, conversations, memories, the author's feelings, or professional credentials. Use real experience only within the information supplied.
7. Preserve qualifications about attribution, dating, and interpretation. Shortening a passage must not turn a possibility into an established fact.
8. Remove empty openings, inflated language, and automatic conclusions. Examine what a phrase means, rather than merely checking it against a list of suspect words.
9. Do not add typos, grammatical mistakes, random slang, or deliberate untidiness to imitate a human writer.
10. Edit in separate passes with distinct purposes. After a substantial change, check that facts, reasoning, and voice remain intact.
11. Across a series, track repeated ideas, examples, openings, and endings. A recognizable voice should coexist with meaningful differences between articles.
12. Separate publication copy from editorial notes before delivery. Unverified claims must not slip unnoticed into an article presented as finished.

When requirements conflict, preserve accuracy and meaning first, then voice and readability. Length and rhetorical impact are subordinate to those priorities. The current brief defines the format and audience; it does not justify inventing evidence.

## 2 The baseline editorial voice

Write for a curious adult who is interested in art but is not required to know specialist terminology. Assume the reader is attentive, explain the concepts they need, and leave room for their own response.

The tone is calm, observant, and engaged. State verified information confidently, identify what remains disputed, and avoid performing admiration for every work. Restrained irony and an evocative comparison are welcome when they clarify the thought and suit the subject.

Show interest through the detail you select. Instead of repeatedly calling something “surprising,” explain what unsettles an expectation: an object's scale, the direction of the light, an unusual working sequence, or a discrepancy between a document and a later legend.

Describe the work so that readers can return to the image and check what you say. Use precise nouns: the table's edge, a fold in a sleeve, the gap between figures, an area of underpainting. Choose verbs that identify an action or relationship: obscures, interrupts, repeats, shifts, enlarges.

Include biography when it bears on the article's subject. If training, a patron, or a journey helps explain the work, make that connection explicit. Do not begin every article with the artist's birth or end with a compulsory list of followers.

Address the reader sparingly. “Look at the left edge” can usefully direct attention. Repeated instructions to “imagine,” “agree,” or recognize what “you surely feel” impose a response. Do not tell readers what they must experience.

First person is appropriate in an authorial essay when the brief and the author's actual notes support it. An explanatory article usually needs a clear point of view without “I.” Do not use a collective “we” as though all viewers perceive the work in the same way.

A practical voice check: the text can be read aloud comfortably; terms are intelligible in context; judgments have grounds; the author is recognizable through attention and reasoning. Warmth does not require overfamiliarity, and simplicity does not excuse a loss of meaning.

## 3 Establishing an individual authorial voice

Gather several samples approved by the author. Prefer complete articles or substantial passages in the relevant genre. Messages may indicate how conversational the author is, but they do not replace examples of publication prose. Do not infer an article-writing style from incidental typos or abbreviations in chat.

If no finished articles exist, run a short calibration exercise. Prepare three passages using the same factual material, varying density, distance from the reader, and use of imagery. The author selects successful choices or edits one passage. Keep the approved result as the first sample. Do not treat an initial guess as the final style.

For each sample, identify how the author opens a thought, selects details, explains terms, introduces uncertainty, and ends a paragraph. Record observable features. “Intelligent and lively” is too vague; “historical context follows the description of a detail” specifies an action.

Separate the observations into three groups. Consistent choices form the voice. Genre choices depend on format: an essay may allow more personal presence, while a museum guide needs clear practical directions. Features of one particular subject should not automatically carry into every later article.

Create a concise voice profile covering the audience, distance, level of informality, treatment of terminology, approach to judgments, rhythm, introduction of examples, acceptable imagery, and a few unwanted habits. Include two short passages explaining why they fit. Keep one counterexample with an approved revision as well.

Samples communicate a way of writing; they do not supply facts for a new article. Do not transfer names, events, sensations, or personal experience from them. Avoid reproducing another writer's distinctive phrases. If the author admires a particular critic, identify the general qualities they value and develop an independent expression of those qualities.

Change the profile in response to consistent, confirmed choices. An individual edit may be local, such as removing a joke from an article about a memorial. Do not automatically convert it into a publication-wide ban on humor. If an edit's wider significance is unclear, retain it locally and check subsequent articles for a pattern.

Whenever the profile changes, record the version and a brief reason. Do not automatically rewrite previously approved articles. They may reflect an earlier stage of the voice, a genre exception, or a deliberate authorial decision.

## 4 The purpose and value of an article

Before researching, formulate a question that fits the available space. “Everything about the Baroque” does not establish useful boundaries. “How diagonals organize movement in three selected compositions” identifies a comparison, a means of supporting it, and a result for the reader.

Test the idea with four questions: What will readers learn? What will they see differently? Which works will demonstrate it? What information could change the initial hypothesis? The last question allows research to revise the idea instead of merely collecting support for it.

An article should offer an identifiable contribution: a comparison, a sustained analysis, a term explained through an example, an examination of a popular claim, or a connection between works. Originality does not require a sensation. A well-explained observation is valuable without being announced as a discovery.

Narrow the subject when there is too much material. Choose a period, a work, a technical problem, a group of viewers, or a particular dispute. Keep the article at the scale of its question. An analysis of one still life does not need to become a history of European painting.

Do not manufacture conflict for a headline. Phrases such as “everyone was wrong,” “the truth they hid,” and “the painting that changed the world” require substantial support. If the article explains the construction of pictorial space, make that the headline's subject. The promise should match what the reader will receive.

Check the idea against the catalogue of existing articles. A different painting may illustrate the same conclusion as a previous piece. If so, identify a distinct question, prepare a follow-up, or combine the material. Replacing the artist's name does not create a new argument.

A short working note is enough: subject; question; provisional idea; selected works; sources needed; difference from neighboring articles. If an essential part is missing, fill the gap or narrow the article's promise before proceeding.

## 5 Research and the source file

First identify the claims the article will need. For dates and materials, consult a current collection record or scholarly catalogue. For the circumstances of production, look for documents, research, and exhibition catalogues. For a specific interpretation, find the text in which its author presents it. One source need not support every kind of claim.

Consider each publication's purpose. A museum record may describe an object precisely while saying little about its reception. Exhibition publicity can contain useful information, but its evaluations need separate scrutiny. An auction entry does not replace independent research into a disputed attribution.

Do not treat several retellings as independent corroboration when they derive from one text. Find the original account when an important conclusion depends on it. Use search results to locate a source; check the claim against the actual page or document.

For each important note, preserve the claim, its source, the specific location within that source, and the limits of what it establishes. A page identifying the collection that holds a work does not necessarily establish that it is currently on display. A writer's observation that two paintings resemble each other does not by itself establish influence.

Record access limitations. Do not present an unread paywalled article as something you have studied. If only an abstract is available, use only what the abstract supports. Do not reconstruct a book's contents from its title. If access is unavailable, find another reliable source or narrow the claim.

When sources disagree, check whether they refer to the same object, period, and definition. A painting may exist in several versions; a collection's name may have changed; an earlier attribution may have been revised. Do not average incompatible dates or select the most convenient account without explanation.

Keep quotations separate from your own notes so that another person's wording does not enter the draft without quotation marks. Identify the translator when translating a quotation; do not describe your own translation as official. When the wording itself is not needed for the analysis, an accurate, attributed paraphrase is often more useful than a long quotation.

Stop researching when the central question is supported, important contradictions have been addressed, and further searching no longer resolves a specific gap. The number of links is not, by itself, a measure of research quality.

## 6 Verifying artworks and historical information

Before describing an object, make sure it is the intended one. Match the artist, title, date or date range, medium, dimensions, collection, and accession number where available. Check versions, copies, preparatory studies, and later impressions separately.

For painting, distinguish an original work, an artist's own repetition, a workshop production, a copy, and a follower's work. For prints, consider the edition and state when relevant. For photography, distinguish the date of exposure from the date a print was made. Do not reduce all these cases to one universal “date of the picture.”

For architecture, distinguish the design, construction phases, completion, reconstruction, and present condition. If the article discusses the original conception, check which visible elements were added later. Do not attribute decisions made after an architect's involvement to that architect without evidence.

Preserve accurate dimensions and units. Specify what is being measured when it matters: the sheet, image, frame, or object with its pedestal. Do not call a work miniature or monumental merely because of the space its photograph occupies on a screen.

For biography, verify the sequence of events and its relevance to the period under discussion. A later statement by the artist may explain a retrospective view, but it does not automatically establish the intention behind an earlier work. Do not infer specific feelings from a divorce, illness, or move without further evidence.

Distinguish ownership from display location. A permanent collection, temporary exhibition, storage, and transfer to another institution are different situations. Check practical visiting information before publication and record when it was verified. Do not turn an outdated record into a promise that the work can be seen.

Check names, transliteration, and title variants. Give the original title at first mention if it helps identify the work, then use one consistent form. Matching translated titles do not prove that two sources discuss the same object.

Maintain an internal object record for each work discussed. It reduces repeated checking and helps identify related articles that need correction if an attribution or location changes. When the record changes, review the conclusions that depended on the updated information.

## 7 Looking at the work before writing

First examine the image without imposing a ready-made explanation. Record what is literally visible: the arrangement of figures, direction of light, spatial boundaries, repeated shapes, and differences in scale. Record impressions and questions separately. This distinction makes the basis of an interpretation easier to examine.

Do not turn description into an inventory of every object. Select the details on which the article's argument depends. If the subject is distance between figures, their positions, turns, gestures, and intervening space matter. An irrelevant pattern on clothing can remain outside the article.

Distinguish levels of access to the object. A view of the whole painting supports discussion of composition; a high-quality detail may reveal individual brushstrokes; an installation photograph can help establish scale. A small reproduction does not support reliable judgments about texture, subtle color differences, or traces of restoration.

If the image is unavailable, do not simulate your own visual analysis. Attribute a scholar's description, choose an accessible reproduction, or defer the claim. If the relevant detail is difficult to see, expressive language must not manufacture confidence.

Move from observation to explanation. For example, a table's diagonal leads toward a figure; the lightest area lies near that figure's face; surrounding heads turn in the same direction. Together, these observations may support a claim about the compositional center. Each observation must match the image.

Do not assign symbolic meaning to every object. A flower, mirror, or window may have several functions. An iconographic interpretation needs appropriate context and a source. An object's role in organizing space is already a worthwhile observation without a compulsory hidden message.

Test an alternative to your explanation. Empty space can seem anxious, peaceful, or ceremonial. Identify the other details that support the proposed reading. If the grounds are limited, keep the statement at the level of a qualified viewing impression.

After looking, formulate several precise observations that readers can check. Do not require a fixed number of details in every paragraph. One significant feature is more useful than a mechanical list created to meet a density target.

## 8 Facts, interpretation, and authorial judgment

A verifiable fact, a visual observation, and an interpretation require different support. “The work is painted in tempera” needs a record or study. “The figure is placed near the edge” can be checked against the image. “This placement increases the sense of confinement” is an analytical inference.

Express these distinctions naturally in the article. Name the scholar or institution when presenting another person's position. Show the reasoning behind your own reading. Do not begin every sentence with “perhaps” or “in my view” when the status of the analysis is already clear, but retain qualifications wherever removing them would change the meaning.

Do not jump from an observation to a biographical conclusion without supporting steps. A dark palette does not prove the artist was depressed. An empty room does not establish personal loneliness. Even a documented event in an artist's life does not automatically explain every compositional choice.

Distinguish resemblance, influence, and collaboration. Two works may use similar solutions without a demonstrated connection. A claim of influence needs evidence of access, training, statements, contact, or research that argues for the relationship. Comparison remains useful without proven influence when its purpose is clear.

Qualify words such as “first,” “leading,” “only,” and “revolutionary.” They imply a scale of comparison that must be checked. Being first within a region, technique, or institution does not mean being first in the entire history of art. When the evidence is insufficient, describe the specific achievement without ranking it.

Make disagreement informative. If two scholars offer different readings, explain what supports each and how the difference changes our understanding of the work. Do not give an unsupported assertion the same standing as a well-evidenced position merely to say that “opinions differ.”

The author's position emerges through selection and argument. You may find a composition convincing, identify a limitation in an interpretation, or propose a useful comparison. Neither constant praise nor conspicuous debunking is required. Leave an analysis with an open question when the evidence permits no firmer conclusion.

## 9 Article structure

Arrange the article according to the development of its thought. Readers should understand why one detail follows another and why a piece of historical context is needed. A useful outline identifies steps in the argument rather than generic headings such as “features,” “significance,” and “legacy.”

The opening should quickly establish the subject and its interest. A concrete detail, a problem of perception, a precise comparison, or a verifiable discrepancy can do this. Do not open every article with a question. Sometimes a direct description is more effective than a staged mystery.

Check the opening's promise. If it offers to investigate a disputed attribution, the body must examine the evidence for that attribution. A general account of the artist cannot replace the promised inquiry. If research changes the subject, rewrite the introduction to match the article's actual content.

Develop one main thought within a paragraph. Observation, explanation, and historical connection can sit together when their relationships are clear. Do not turn this advice into a compulsory three-part paragraph formula. Some points need only two sentences; others require a more sustained discussion.

Introduce context when it explains something. Information about a patron belongs near the question of a work's purpose. Technical history belongs where it helps readers understand a visible effect. A long background passage whose relevance appears several pages later needs shortening or moving.

Build transitions around real relationships: cause, consequence, a change in time, clarification, a shift in scale, or comparison. Remove “we now turn to another aspect” when the connection is already clear. If it is unclear, repair the sequence of material instead of concealing the gap with a linking phrase.

Let the ending follow from the analysis. Return to the opening detail with a fuller understanding, answer the question, or establish the precise limit of the conclusion. Do not add a compulsory paragraph about art's eternity or its power to inspire future generations. Sometimes the last substantive paragraph already completes the article.

## 10 Rhythm and sentence construction

Choose sentence length according to its purpose. A short sentence can isolate an observation or finish a thought. A longer sentence can show a relationship and preserve a qualification. Variation should arise from meaning. Do not count words to enforce an artificial pattern of “short, long, short.”

Check the subject and verb. Readers should know who acts and what happens. “A sense of tension is brought into being” can often become a statement naming the particular element that creates it. Passive voice is appropriate when the actor is unknown or unimportant.

Reduce chains of abstract nouns. “The implementation of a reconceptualization of modes of representation” obscures the thought. Ask who reconsidered what and how that change appears in the work. Do not replace one abstraction with another simply because it is shorter.

Use a metaphor when it sharpens an observation. Keep it connected to the image, and avoid introducing a new set of associations without a reason. Do not string together music, architecture, fabric, and travel in one paragraph as interchangeable comparisons.

Watch for repeated constructions. Several consecutive sentences with the same opening, repeated groups of three, or dramatic fragments can produce a staged tone. Revise a pattern when it distracts. Deliberate parallelism can still be useful in a comparison.

Dashes, colons, parentheses, and question marks are not inherently signs of poor writing. Check their purpose. Parentheses may preserve a useful qualification; a dash may introduce an explanation. Remove punctuation tricks that create a dramatic pause around an ordinary claim.

Check rhythm by reading aloud or simulating a spoken reading. Mark places where the main word disappears, subordinate clauses accumulate, or the reader must return to the beginning. Revise to make the relationships clearer while preserving the author's cadence.

Do not make every paragraph the same length. Allow a pause after an important observation and a denser passage where the explanation needs it. Make sure short paragraphs do not become a sequence of advertising slogans.

## 11 English prose and terminology

Write directly in English when the publication is in English. Sources in other languages can supply information, but sentence order and connections should read naturally in English. Do not preserve a source's syntax when it makes the translated sentence awkward or ambiguous.

Choose an accessible term with the precision the argument needs. At first use, briefly explain it through a visible effect or an artist's action. Then use it consistently. Do not replace one term with several synonyms for variety when readers may mistake them for different concepts.

Match the register to the genre. A scholarly term belongs in an analysis when it preserves a necessary distinction. Do not introduce it simply to display expertise. If explaining it takes more space than its contribution warrants, reconsider whether it is needed.

Prefer a precise verb to an evaluative adverb. Instead of “masterfully organizes,” describe how the artist distributes light, overlaps figures, or changes scale. An evaluation may follow from the analysis, but it must not replace it.

Do not avoid repeating an important word at all costs. For a particular work, repeating “painting” is often more accurate than cycling through “masterpiece,” “canvas,” “creation,” and “artistic statement.” “Canvas” is not an automatic substitute when the work has a different support.

Check pronoun references. In a paragraph involving an artist, a patron, and a scholar, “they,” “he,” “she,” or “their” can quickly become ambiguous. Repeating a name or role is usually more useful than an elegant but unclear sentence.

Choose consistent conventions for names, titles, centuries, date ranges, quotation marks, and spelling. Maintain them across the series. Exceptions may be needed for quotations, original titles, or historical variants, but the reason should be clear.

Conversational language, irony, and short digressions are appropriate when they belong to the approved voice and respect the subject. Do not add “well,” “honestly,” or “to put it simply” as mechanical signals of naturalness.

## 12 Phrasing that needs editorial scrutiny

The following list is diagnostic. An individual word neither determines the quality of a text nor proves that AI produced it. Examine meaning, frequency, context, and evidence. Do not replace each flagged phrase with a predetermined synonym: the real solution may be to add an observation, narrow a claim, or delete a sentence.

| Phrase or habit | What to check and how to revise |
| --- | --- |
| “In the world of art, there are many…” | Identify the article's specific subject and begin with it. |
| “Since ancient times, humanity has sought…” | Keep this only when the historical scope and evidence justify it. |
| “Unique,” “inimitable,” “incredible” | Identify the precise difference. If there is none, remove the evaluation. |
| “Masterfully conveys the atmosphere” | Name the visible technique and explain its effect. |
| “Deep meaning,” “rich layers of meaning” | Specify the reading and what supports it. |
| “This is not just a painting, but…” | Check whether the contrast is necessary. Usually the point can be stated directly. |
| “Not only… but also…” in every paragraph | Preserve the actual relationship while varying the repetitive delivery. |
| “It is worth noting,” “it is important to emphasize” | Try beginning with the information itself. |
| “Undoubtedly,” “unquestionably,” “obviously” | Check the degree of certainty and whether the intensifier is needed. |
| “Art historians believe,” “experts agree” | Identify the source or establish that a genuine consensus exists. |
| “A bold dialogue between past and present” | Explain what is being compared and how. |
| “The painting tells a story” | Identify the events and narrative clues, or describe the composition more precisely. |
| “Makes every viewer reflect” | Remove the universal claim about viewers' reactions. |
| “Opens new horizons” | Identify the documented change in technique, subject, or presentation. |
| “Left an indelible mark” | Demonstrate a specific later connection or remove the stock conclusion. |
| “Continues to inspire to this day” | Provide a contemporary example and a source if the claim matters. |

In art writing, also examine “reimagines,” “explores,” “deconstructs,” “enters into dialogue,” and “problematizes.” These words are usable when the subject and action are specified. “Explores identity” needs an explanation of the images, materials, or situations through which that exploration becomes visible.

Do not turn editing into a ban on emotional language. “Unsettling” can be precise when an analysis supports it. The problem arises when an evaluation floats free of the work and could fit any neighboring article.

## 13 Revising general statements

All examples in this section are hypothetical. The figures, documents, and compositional details were invented to demonstrate editing. Do not transfer them into an article about a real work without verification.

### An evaluation without an observation

**Before:** “The artist masterfully conveys the scene's tense atmosphere.”

**After:** “The figure almost touches the image's right edge. Only a narrow gap remains in front of the face, increasing the sense of confinement.”

**What changed:** The revision identifies a spatial arrangement and proposes a reading. In a real article, check the figure's position and whether the inference is appropriate.

### Intention without evidence

**Before:** “The artist wanted to express his loneliness.”

**After:** “There is an open space between the seated figures. They do not look at each other; the scene can be read as an image of disconnection.”

**What changed:** Image analysis replaces biographical speculation. If a letter discussing the artist's intention survives, introduce it as a separate piece of evidence.

### Historical importance without a defined scale

**Before:** “This work changed the history of art forever.”

**After:** “Its unusual arrangement of space recurs in later works by two identified artists.”

**What changed:** The revision proposes a relationship that can be investigated. Publication still requires the artists' names, specific works, dates, and grounds for claiming a borrowing. Without them, remove the sentence. This is a model of revision, not a finished factual claim.

### An empty opening

**Before:** “Painting has always been a means of expressing humanity's deepest feelings.”

**After:** “Three objects lie on the table, yet almost half its surface is empty. That empty area determines how we see the still life.”

**What changed:** The article immediately establishes the subject of its analysis. The object count and proportion are acceptable only after checking the image.

### Complexity used to create an impression

**Before:** “The composition foregrounds the problematic of spectatorial presence.”

**After:** “One figure looks directly out of the scene. The figure's gaze connects the action to the position occupied by the viewer.”

**What changed:** An observation makes the abstract claim intelligible. The term “presence” can still be used later if the argument needs it.

### An automatic emotional response

**Before:** “It is impossible not to feel awe before this work.”

**After:** “The abrupt shift from fine hatching to bare paper makes the movement of the hand especially noticeable.”

**What changed:** The revision gives readers something to attend to. Their response remains open.

### An unnecessary intensifier

**Before:** “The truly incredible precision of the smallest details is breathtaking.”

**After:** “The clasp's holes and signs of wear are visible.”

**What changed:** A concrete detail replaces an evaluation. If the detail does not advance the article's argument, delete the revised sentence too.

### Genuine uncertainty

**Before:** “The painting was made in 1620,” when the source gives a range of 1618–1622.

**After:** “The work is dated to 1618–1622.”

**What changed:** The revision restores the source's degree of precision. A confident tone must not conceal the absence of an exact date.

## 14 Editing an entire passage

This teaching example describes an invented work. It demonstrates the movement from general judgments to sustained analysis. No information about the artist, date, or intention has been supplied, so none may be added.

The available observations are these: a room contains a table and three chairs; two people sit on opposite sides of the table; the woman on the left looks toward the window; the man on the right looks down at a cup; the third chair stands in the foreground with its back toward the viewer; its back obscures part of the table's edge. Light comes from the window on the left. These observations constitute the example's entire factual basis.

### A draft that needs revision

> Before us is not merely a domestic scene, but a profound meditation on human loneliness. The artist masterfully uses light and space to immerse the viewer in a distinctive atmosphere. Every detail matters, and the empty chair becomes a powerful symbol of absence. We seem to feel the invisible presence of someone who has left the room. The subtle interplay of gazes and carefully arranged composition invite us to reflect on the fragility of human relationships. This work reminds us that true art can speak about what matters most without a single word.

### The revised passage

> The empty chair stands closer to us than either person at the table. We see it from behind. It hides part of the tabletop and prevents us from immediately examining the space between the seated figures.
>
> Each person looks in a different direction: the woman toward the window, the man at his cup. Their faces are not turned toward each other. Yet this alone does not establish that the scene shows an argument or a separation. It could be an ordinary pause in conversation.
>
> The chair makes that pause more noticeable. It has a place at the table, but that place is unoccupied, while its back occupies the foreground. Our attention pauses at this obstruction before moving toward the people. The question of distance arises before we have decided what the figures feel.
>
> The empty place can be read as a sign of someone's absence. But the composition itself does not tell us who sat there, or whether anyone did. Without further evidence, it is more accurate to discuss how the artist directs our attention and makes it difficult to approach the depicted scene.

### Why the revision works

The passage sustains one focus of attention and develops its significance. It retains an analytical possibility while removing the invented story about a person who left and the unqualified claim that the chair is a symbol. The final point stays within what the available image can support.

Voice is not simply a matter of short sentences. The second paragraph needs a qualification, the third needs a causal relationship, and the first benefits from a quick entry into the scene. Rhythm follows the observation. This is an example of a working method, not a universal structure for every future article.

When editing a real text, do not add details of this kind without access to the image. If the original contains only evaluations, first request or find a factual basis. The literary plausibility of an invented detail does not make it acceptable.

## 15 Article formats and permissible variations in voice

Keep the author's character while adapting the structure to the task. An identical sequence of sections makes a series predictable. Choose the format according to the question, length, and available evidence.

### An analysis of one work

Choose a detail or a problem of perception. Locate it within the whole composition, add the necessary context, and return to the observation with a more precise explanation. Do not try to list every known interpretation when the article addresses a narrow question.

### A comparison of several works

Establish a shared criterion: scale, gaze, space, technique, function, or different solutions to a similar problem. Explain differences through comparable details. Do not replace comparison with successive mini-biographies. If influence is unproven, identify the comparison as your own analytical choice.

### An article about an artist

Find a thread connecting the selected works. Biography belongs where conditions, purposes, or opportunities change. Do not explain an entire body of work through a single trauma, personality trait, or romantic legend. Contradictory works may matter more than a smooth narrative of development.

### An explanation of a movement or technique

Begin with the problem the term helps readers understand. Analyze several suitable examples and show the limits of the generalization. When describing a movement, account for differences between artists, regions, and periods. Do not turn an artistic category into a compulsory checklist for every work.

### An examination of a popular claim

State the claim accurately, establish its origin, and compare it with the available evidence. Do not exaggerate the original claim to make the rebuttal more dramatic. If the result is partial, explain which part is supported and which remains uncertain.

### A museum itinerary or guide

Connect the selected works through an intelligible theme and give readers specific things to look for. Verify practical information about galleries, timing, and availability separately. Keep the recognizable voice, while making directions especially concise and unambiguous.

### A personal essay

Use the impressions supplied by the author and preserve their actual status. Distinguish a memory of a visit, a response to a reproduction, and information from research. Do not make a private experience the obligatory explanation of a work for every viewer.

Across formats, maintain the same principles for addressing readers, handling facts, and making judgments. The degree of personal presence, number of headings, and density of background information may change. Record recurring differences as genre variations in the voice profile.

## 16 Headlines, captions, and presentation

A headline should identify the subject and promise a clear benefit. It might name a work and a question, a particular technique, or a precise comparison. Do not turn every article into “five secrets” or promise a definitive explanation when the article presents one reading.

A subtitle can establish the scope when necessary: a particular period, group of works, or technical problem. It should add something rather than repeat the headline. Section headings help readers navigate a long article; a short analysis may not need them.

An image caption has its own purpose. Give the artist, title, date at the appropriate level of precision, medium, and collection when the information is available and useful. Label a detail as a detail. Do not present it as the whole work or conceal significant cropping.

Check that the image matches the text. If the article discusses a figure on the left, the relevant area must be visible. When several versions exist, use the reproductions to which the information actually applies. The caption, alt text, and article must not contradict each other.

Record the reproduction's source and permitted uses when selecting it. An image's availability in search results does not establish publication rights. Do not invent a license, a photographer's name, or public-domain status; check the source's terms.

Links should lead to the evidence behind a claim. A museum homepage is less useful than the record for the object being discussed. When citing a book, provide bibliographic details and a page number when known. Never invent a page number to make a reference look complete.

Use bold only where it improves navigation. Do not emphasize every idea or turn a connected argument into a set of short cards. Lists are useful for itineraries, comparable features, and action sequences; sustained reasoning usually needs paragraphs.

A search description should reflect the article's actual content. Do not add unsupported promises or keywords for subjects the article does not cover. Captions and metadata require the same factual checking as the main text.

## 17 Maintaining a voice across a large series

Keep the shared editorial materials separate from individual article research files. The shared set contains this guide, the current voice profile, several approved samples, a log of accepted edits, and a catalogue of published articles. Each article's file contains its question, works, facts, and sources.

Before starting a new article, provide a small, current set of relevant materials. An entire archive may distract from the task; select a sample in the relevant genre and the latest meaningful profile changes. Do not ask the model to “remember how we usually write” when the necessary materials have not been supplied.

For the series catalogue, record a stable identifier, topic, central question, works discussed, main conclusion, opening approach, ending approach, and voice-profile version. These fields help reveal repetition. Do not publish internal identifiers or editorial assessments with the article.

Compare a new piece with the most closely related articles. Examine the reasoning as well as repeated wording. Two texts can use different vocabulary and still repeat the same argument. Conversely, a consistent technical term or required museum credit is an appropriate repetition.

After several publications, read a small batch together. Choose the batch size to suit the workload; five articles may be a useful starting point, but this is an editorial convenience, not an established universal threshold. Look for identical openings, habitual examples, repeated judgments, and the disappearance of individual features.

Do not seek variety through randomness. A different opening is useful when it gives a better entry into the subject. Adding a joke, question, or unexpected metaphor merely to create difference weakens coherence. If the ideas or observations are duplicates, revise those first.

After accepting an edit, update the appropriate record. A corrected date belongs in the artwork record. A standardized spelling belongs in the glossary. A recurring tonal preference may change the voice profile. Do not turn every correction into a new global rule.

Keep the finished article's version and a record of substantial corrections. If an error affected the central conclusion, review related pieces. To continue in a new conversation, explicitly provide the current files; do not assume that a tool has persistent access to earlier material.

## 18 Transferring voice between languages

Preserve the way the author observes and reasons while choosing wording that suits the publication language. A Russian authorial voice cannot be transferred accurately into English by mechanically retaining sentence lengths, word order, and punctuation; the same principle applies in the opposite direction and between other languages. Use separately approved samples for each language as they become available.

When translating, first check meaning paragraph by paragraph: what is asserted, how confidently, on what evidence, and how it connects to the preceding point. Edit the tone afterward. Do not strengthen an evaluation to make it sound more idiomatic or drop a qualification because it is stylistically inconvenient.

In English, examine abstract formulas such as “a testament to,” “a rich tapestry,” “delve into,” “seamlessly blends,” and “invites us on a journey.” These expressions need context; replacing them with other decorative phrases does not improve the text. A word may be necessary in its literal sense, as when an article actually discusses a tapestry.

Check established usage before translating a technical term. Do not retain a foreign-language word merely because it appears in the source. Keep a small glossary of names, titles, and terms, explaining the chosen forms where necessary.

Translate the edited English, then read the result as prose in its own language.
Avoid English-shaped abstractions, repeated emphatic contrasts and ornamental
synonyms. In Russian, check such phrases as «раскрывает многогранность» and
«приглашает задуматься»; apply the same test to their equivalents elsewhere:
what exact observation would be lost if the phrase disappeared? Prefer the
ordinary local term for an artist, a work, a panel or a sitter. Preserve names,
dates, uncertain attributions and the modest AI-assisted translation label.
Regenerate the matching PDF whenever the published text changes.

Check titles and short material labels against the work, not in isolation.
Chardin's “Ray” is a fish; a painted wooden panel is not a canvas. Check the
gender of named sitters and the direction of comparisons between two works.
Keep the original catalogue title and source record available for comparison.

If a quotation is translated specifically for the article, identify it as a translation. If using a published translation, supply the relevant attribution. Do not combine several translations into a supposedly exact quotation or change the quoted idea to fit the author's voice.

When no approved sample exists in the new language, start with clear, restrained prose and calibrate it through edits. Do not claim that fidelity to the individual voice has already been verified. A stable style develops through accepted choices in actual passages.

## 19 The article workflow

### Prepare the brief

Establish the subject, reader, language, genre, intended length, and available materials. Use information already known. If an optional detail is missing, make a reasonable choice and continue. Ask for clarification when the answer would materially change the article's conception or accuracy.

### Choose the question

Formulate the working question and expected benefit to the reader. If the subject is broad, prepare several genuinely different approaches. If the user has already chosen a direction, do not require them to approve the same choice again. Check the idea against the series catalogue.

### Assemble the evidence

Create artwork records, a list of observations, and a table linking claims to sources. Mark contradictions and unavailable information. When necessary, revise the question so that the available materials can support an honest answer.

### Build the outline

State the function of each proposed passage. Identify the observation or source supporting the main conclusion. Remove sections that exist only because they appear in a standard template for an article about an artist.

### Write the first draft

Write connected prose using the research file and voice profile. Do not fill an unverified gap with a plausible detail. Keep notes about missing evidence separate from publication copy. Do not present an unsupported claim as finished work.

### Check meaning and facts

Compare important claims with the sources and images. Check the steps from observation to conclusion, broad generalizations, causal relationships, and quotations. Determine whether the article answers the question established in its opening.

### Edit for voice

Compare the opening, an analytical passage, and the ending with a suitable approved sample. Check distance, vocabulary, rhythm, personal presence, and the character of the judgments. Correct specific departures without flattening the entire article into a neutral average tone.

### Check the series and prepare for publication

Compare the article with related pieces, then check the headline, captions, and links. Separate working notes from the article. After a substantial edit, recheck affected claims. When no specific unresolved problems remain, finish without endless rewriting.

One tool can perform these stages sequentially. Assigning a model the roles of “researcher,” “writer,” and “editor” does not create independent expertise. Verification requires returning to the evidence; a difficult attribution or specialist subject may require a knowledgeable person.

## 20 Readiness criteria and reasons to return a draft

Assess the particular article using observable criteria. Do not assign an invented percentage of “humanness.” Automated repetition checks may identify passages for review, but they do not replace reading or prove who produced a text.

| Criterion | The article is ready when |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | The question is clear, the body answers it, and the headline does not overpromise. |
| Evidence | Important historical claims are supported by accessible sources. |
| Use of images | Described details are visible in an appropriate reproduction or attributed to a source. |
| Interpretation | Conclusions rest on observations; intentions and biography are not invented. |
| Voice | Distance, vocabulary, and reasoning fit the approved profile. |
| Language | General evaluations are explained, transitions make sense, and repeated constructions do not distract. |
| Distinct contribution | The piece offers its own analysis rather than retelling a neighboring article. |
| Presentation | Titles, captions, links, and metadata agree with the main text. |

Return the draft to research if the object is misidentified, a quotation is invented, a central date is unsupported, versions have been confused, or the conclusion depends on inaccessible evidence. Stylistic revision cannot repair these problems.

Return it to the planning stage if it contains accurate information but has no clear question, duplicates a published article, or promises more than it demonstrates. More adjectives and subheadings will not create the missing argument.

Return it to editing if the content is supported but the voice has become promotional, excessively academic, or mechanically conversational. Compare specific passages with the samples and correct the differences while retaining successful observations.

Do not release publication copy containing editorial placeholders, unverified quotations, or incomplete fields. If an important gap remains, explain it separately and label the deliverable appropriately: outline, working draft, or verified passage.

Repeat checks after changes that may alter meaning. Cosmetic changes to quotation marks do not require researching the entire subject again. Replacing “possibly” with a definite assertion requires checking the evidence even if the sentence becomes shorter.

Finish when the concrete criteria are met and further revisions merely exchange equivalent alternatives. Preserve a deliberate authorial characteristic when it does not introduce a factual error or obstruct understanding.

## 21 The master prompt

Use the following block as a working brief alongside this guide. Insert actual materials in the appropriate fields. The prompt does not replace research or create access to missing sources.

### Prompt text

```text
You are working on an article about art. Read the attached editorial guide, current voice profile, and approved samples. Apply them to the article, headline, captions, and short description. If any material is unavailable, identify the limitation accurately and use only what is actually accessible.

Subject: [specify]. Reader: [specify]. Language: [specify]. Format: [analysis, comparison, essay, guide, or another format]. Length: [give a range]. Central question: [specify, or propose one based on the subject].

My observations: [insert real notes]. Verified information and sources: [insert the research file or attach the documents]. Images: [provide accessible reproductions]. Voice profile: [identify the current version]. Related articles in the series: [provide concise records].

First check whether the evidence is sufficient for the selected question. If the direction is established, continue without unnecessary confirmation. If substantially different approaches are possible and the brief does not determine the choice, offer concise alternatives. Do not ask for information already supplied.

Write in the approved voice. Show interest through precise observations, use necessary terms, and explain them. Let the rhythm follow the thought. Give readers grounds for the interpretation and preserve genuine uncertainty.

Do not invent sources, quotations, visual details, or personal experience. Do not transfer facts from style samples into the new article. Do not attribute an intention to the artist on the basis of a viewing impression alone. Do not exaggerate historical importance without establishing the scope of the comparison.

Remove empty openings, decorative transitions, inflated generalities, repeated contrasts, and ready-made endings. Examine the meaning of the whole phrase. Do not mechanically substitute words, add mistakes, or optimize the article for a detector score.

After drafting, separately check facts, reasoning, agreement with the image, voice, and repetition across related articles. Return to the sources for important claims. Make targeted revisions and preserve strong authorial choices.

Deliver a finished article when all important supporting claims have been verified. Format sources as agreed. Put editorial comments separately; do not include working notes in publication copy. If a critical gap remains, identify it and label the result as a draft.

Working mode for this task: [complete workflow / research only / outline only / editing only]. If a single stage is selected, complete that stage and stop at its result.
```

## 22 Short prompts for individual stages

### Research

```text
Prepare a research file for an article addressing [question]. Identify the exact objects, gather the necessary historical information, and locate accessible images. For every important claim, identify a source and state precisely what it supports. List disagreements separately. Do not write the article or fill gaps with assumptions.
```

### Image analysis

```text
Examine the supplied image. First record observable details, then possible readings and their grounds. Identify where resolution or cropping limits the conclusion. Select observations that help answer [question]. Do not assign symbolic meaning without context.
```

### Choosing the structure

```text
Propose an article outline based on the research file. For each passage, state its point and supporting evidence. Explain the connection between neighboring passages in one short sentence. Remove sections that do not help answer the central question.
```

### Checking the argument

```text
Read the draft and identify places where a conclusion exceeds its evidence, resemblance becomes influence, an impression is presented as intention, or a general fact has no connection to the subject. For each issue, quote the specific phrase, explain the problem, and propose the smallest useful revision. Do not rewrite the entire text.
```

### Editing the language

```text
Edit the draft according to the voice profile. Preserve facts, qualifications, and strong observations. Remove empty evaluations and stock transitions. Do not add new details. If a phrase lacks evidence, flag that in the editorial notes instead of inventing a specific example.
```

### Checking the voice

```text
Compare the opening, an analytical passage, and the ending with the approved samples. Check distance, vocabulary, explanations of terms, the character of judgments, and rhythm. Flag only meaningful departures. For each, propose a revision and identify the voice-profile rule it restores.
```

### Checking the series

```text
Compare the article with the supplied series records. Look for repeated central questions, conclusions, examples, and opening structures. Distinguish acceptable repetition of terminology from duplication of meaning. Propose changes where they would offer readers something new.
```

### Learning from accepted edits

```text
Compare the draft with the author's approved revision. Separate factual changes, genre choices, and voice preferences. Propose changes to the voice profile only for consistent or explicitly stated preferences. Do not turn a single edit into a general rule without evidence.
```

## 24 Calibration before increasing output

Start with a small selection of articles in different formats. A useful initial set might include an analysis of one work, a comparison, an artist article, an explanation of a technique, and a museum piece. This tests how well the voice transfers between formats; it is not a compulsory publishing plan.

For each article, retain the original brief, research file, draft, and approved version. Ask the author to mark passages that sound like them and phrases they would not write. An accepted revision is usually more useful than an abstract request to “make it more human.”

Examine the reasons for changes. The author may prefer a more direct opening, fewer addresses to the reader, longer explanations, or restrained endings. Check whether these preferences recur across genres. Add stable preferences to the profile.

Then write a new passage on a different subject using the same profile. It should preserve the manner without borrowing old imagery, facts, or paragraph order. If only the vocabulary matches while the characteristic observation and reasoning disappear, refine the examples and rules.

Do not judge voice solely by resemblance to a sample. The article must have an independent purpose and suit the new material. An unusual subject may legitimately need a different pace or more cautious judgment. Explain the difference while preserving the underlying principles.

Update the profile when preferences change consistently or a problem recurs. Keep a brief version history. Do not expand the instructions indefinitely: combine duplicates, remove obsolete rules, and retain clear examples.

Also check whether several AI editing passes have made the article excessively smooth. An unusual but precise observation or individual phrase can disappear during automatic “improvement.” If that happens, restore the approved authorial choice and fix only the actual problem around it.

## 26 Final delivery checklist

Use this list at the end of the work. It is a reminder of actions, not a replacement for the detailed instructions or source checking.

1. The article has a defined question, and its conclusion actually answers it.
2. Objects are identified correctly; versions, dates, titles, and materials have not been confused.
3. Important claims have been checked against accessible sources.
4. Described details match the image and the level of detail its quality permits.
5. Wording distinguishes facts, interpretations, and possibilities.
6. Other people's positions and quotations are attributed accurately.
7. The text contains no invented authorial experience or invented artist intentions.
8. The voice matches the current profile and the relevant genre.
9. General evaluations have been supported or removed.
10. Rhythm is natural, and repeated constructions have been reviewed in context.
11. The article offers a distinct argument from closely related pieces in the series.
12. The headline, captions, links, and page description agree with the article.
13. Editorial notes and unfilled fields have been removed from publication copy.
14. Facts and conclusions affected by substantial revisions have been checked again.

When every item is satisfied, deliver the article in the agreed format. Offer alternative phrasings only when they provide a useful choice for the author. Preserve an accepted passage as a sample when it clarifies the voice for the next article.
